Things Not To Do With a Dead Body

ABOUT

The piece follows a girl who unexpectedly encounters a dead body and, instead of reacting with fear or respect, begins interacting with it in increasingly strange and inappropriate ways. What starts as curiosity gradually becomes a series of absurd actions that she would obviously never do if someone were watching.

The humor comes from treating something deeply inappropriate as if it were completely normal. The situation becomes more and more ridiculous, while the girl seems increasingly comfortable with crossing boundaries simply because she believes there are no consequences.

At its core, the piece plays with the things people do when they think nobody is watching. It exaggerates the small moments in which we ignore what we know is right or wrong simply because we believe we can get away with it. The dead body becomes an extreme and absurd object through which to explore that idea.

Then, the body suddenly wakes up.

This moment does not necessarily need to have a logical explanation. In fact, the lack of explanation is part of the point. The absurdity of the situation is meant to remain absurd. The body waking up can be interpreted as the moment when the consequences of our actions suddenly become real—when something that seemed harmless because nobody was watching becomes impossible to ignore.

But the piece does not need to tell the audience exactly what it means. It can simply be a ridiculous story about a girl, a dead body, and a series of increasingly questionable decisions. Any deeper interpretation is something the audience can choose to construct afterward.

The absurdity is therefore not just a tool for delivering a message; it is the message's contradiction. We instinctively want to find meaning, logic, or morality in what we watch, even when there may be none. The audience is left wondering whether they were supposed to understand something, laugh at it, feel uncomfortable, or simply accept that something completely nonsensical happened.

Ultimately, the piece asks less "What does this mean?" and more "Why do we feel the need for it to mean something?"
